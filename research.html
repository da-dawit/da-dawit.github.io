Research vision

What does it mean to be intelligent?

Since my first year of university, one question has been behind everything I’ve worked on: what is so different about how humans think compared to robots? Intelligence is hard to define. Like “love,” we nod at the word and move on. But I believe finding the right definition is how we find the next path for robotics.

John Searle’s Chinese Room

Give the room a Chinese sentence, and the machine inside follows a huge rule book to produce a reply. From the outside, the person receiving it thinks, “this machine knows Chinese.”

That is exactly the part we need to figure out. Do robots know the world? Can they separate their own identity and compare it with the environment? Turing tried to answer this with the Turing test.

Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach

My favorite book. Hofstadter says,

Intelligence is defined by the ease of switching between I (Intelligence) and M (Machine) Mode…

What I think robots are missing

An intelligent system can think out of the box, purposely putting itself out of distribution. Most robots today stay in M mode, repeating what they were trained on. So how does a robot get an “I” mode? I think it needs three things.

  1. 01

    Isomorphic thinking

    We decode new knowledge through what we already remember: “1, 2, 3, 4 …” can feel like walking up stairs. A robot should map the world onto its own embodiment the same way, knowing objects and actions by what they do to its body: contact, force, displacement. VLAs trained on demonstrations map pixels to actions instead, and in my tests one that succeeded at the center of a table failed a few centimeters off it. The robot’s own rollouts, through RL or teacher-student training, can help close that gap.

  2. 02

    Intuition

    Kahneman’s System 1: fast heuristics and gut feeling. For a robot, that could be a small forward model that predicts what each candidate action will do to its body and the scene before it moves, cheap enough to run at every step. Large language models in today’s robots mostly name the target or condition visual features. When instructions are scarce or vague, a robot should still infer what to do from its own predictions, without retraining.

  3. 03

    Emerging Emotions

    Emotions could emerge through parent-child relationships, since that is how we learn them: a parent soothes a crying baby until the child can calm itself. In a robot, an emotion could be a slow internal state that sets how boldly it explores, regulated first by a parent model and later by itself. In a full sensor suit, impact becomes pain, strain becomes fatigue, and low battery becomes hunger. Pain should weaken motors and add sensor noise rather than subtract reward, growing as the robot nears breaking, a mild form of death.

With this big dream in hand, I’ve been inserting (some) intelligence into machines!

ROBOTIS, Jun – Aug 2026

Benchmarking 2026 VLA and world models

I benchmarked 2026’s newest VLA and world models (GR00T N1.7, MolmoAct2, VLA-JEPA, and FastWAM) on long-horizon manipulation. LeRobot couldn’t drive OMY directly, so I ran its native policies through the lerobot-robot-ros2-zenoh bridge on a three-camera, 100-demo plate-organizing task, fixing NaN losses, camera and proprioception mismatches, and the gap of a new embodiment.

VLAs such as GR00T N1.7 and MolmoAct2 observe and execute; world-action models such as VLA-JEPA (latent-space prediction) and FastWAM (video diffusion) observe, imagine, choose, and execute. For each, I compared world-model prediction on and off, attention kernels, training length, and full versus partial fine-tuning.

GR00T N1.7 led, with 40% full-task success over 10 trials per plate; MolmoAct2 managed only a partial first grab, and VLA-JEPA and FastWAM did not grasp the first plate. Attention maps showed how much camera placement, cropping, and augmentation matter. Latency made GR00T stutter between action chunks, so I adapted ACT’s temporal ensembling to run asynchronously with age-weighted predictions, raising full-task success from 40% to 60% and smoothing the motion. The evaluations became ROBOTIS’s AI MANIPULATOR #13 video and a technical story in the ROBOTIS docs.

ROBOTIS, Jun – Aug 2026

New policy architectures: Prior-ACT

People often choose how to act at a glance. Prior-ACT builds that into ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers). ACT’s CVAE learns a latent “style” vector from each demonstration but fixes it at zero at inference, so the policy cannot adapt to the scene. Prior-ACT learns a scene prior instead: a frozen DINOv3 ViT-L/16 encodes the image, and a small MLP predicts a distribution over the latent, trained with a KL term toward the CVAE’s latent. At inference, the latent is sampled from this prior, so the robot chooses its behavior from what it sees.

On 10 unseen AI Worker episodes, Prior-ACT’s held-out action error was 13–16% lower than vanilla ACT’s, and it reached ACT’s best in 2.7× fewer training steps; in the AI Worker’s two-bottle pick-and-place on the real robot, it succeeded in 60% of 50 trials against vanilla ACT’s 40% on the same trials. I delivered it to the ROBOTIS Intelligence Team. I also designed an MoE-Diffusion policy and developed Gate-as-Potential, a potential-based shaping method for behavior cloning.

Prior-ACT is still ACT underneath, though. The scene prior picks a behavior mode from the whole image, but nothing tells the policy which object or region matters at the current step. On longer-horizon, harder manipulation, where the relevant part of the scene shifts from one subtask to the next, even slightly out-of-distribution placements broke it.

The language-conditioned VLAs I benchmarked handled this differently. In GR00T N1.7, instruction tokens condition each camera’s attention map, so the same image is read differently depending on the task: language acts as a query that says where to look. A scene prior compresses the image into a single latent and cannot point at a region. This is also why a vision-aligned text encoder such as CLIP or SigLIP matters: the instruction only directs attention well if it lives in the same space as the visual tokens.

ROBOTIS, Jun – Aug 2026

Off-policy reinforcement learning

TurboVLA came out at the end of July 2026, and I tried it as a lighter, language-conditioned alternative to ACT: 17 ms inference and under 1 GB of VRAM. It encodes the instruction with BERT and cross-attends between visual features and the language vectors in both directions. One choice puzzled me: BERT is trained on text alone, so its embeddings are not aligned with images, while an encoder such as CLIP or SigLIP would give vision and language a shared space from the start.

On a bimanual two-bottle pick-and-place on the AI Worker, TurboVLA succeeded in only 5 of 30 trials (17%): although its 90 demos covered the whole square, it only succeeded with bottles within 1-2 cm of the center. To help it generalize, I rebuilt the task in Isaac Sim with physics and cameras calibrated to the real robot, randomized the background, lighting, and bottle placement every episode, trained a small residual actor on the frozen policy with off-policy RL, and transferred it back to the real robot. After RL, it succeeded 70% of the time with bottles 2-3 cm off center and 50% at 4-5 cm.

Since TurboVLA is essentially ACT with language added, I also trained ACT-based policies on the same task as a comparison group, with the same 90 demos and about the same number of training steps. On the real robot, over the same 50 episodes of this two-bottle task, vanilla ACT started at 40% and Prior-ACT at 60%, so I carried Prior-ACT forward. TD3+BC pushed it to 82%, succeeding even with bottles placed in the corners of the square, and in some setups outside its training: the bottles and the basket at the edges of the trained area, or the box raised or lowered.

Both methods build on Physical Intelligence’s RL Token (RLT): the frozen TurboVLA exposes a compact RL token, and a tiny actor-critic (token size 16, 64 hidden units) adds a bounded correction to its action, a = aref + rtrust·tanh(Δ). With TD3+BC, a double-Q critic trains the correction and a BC term keeps it close to the original policy. After sim-to-real transfer, real-robot success rose from 17% to about 30% over 30 trials. On LIBERO-Spatial, online TD3 with the same token raised TurboVLA from 75% to 85% (48 episodes, reproduced), and on LIBERO-10 TD3+BC raised it from 35% to 59% (100 episodes).

The second method, AWR + RECAP, changes only the actor objective. Following RECAP, the method behind Physical Intelligence’s π*0.6, a value function learned from all rollouts, failures included, scores each action by its advantage A = Q(s, ademo) − Q(s, abase), with a −1 per-step reward so faster successes count more, and AWR weights the actions by it. It reached 20% on the real robot (from 17%) and 52% on LIBERO-10 (from 35%).

In the end, neither RL nor more demonstrations made TurboVLA robust. Its action loss converged cleanly, consistent with the training behavior its paper reports, but that did not carry over to bottles even a few centimeters from where they were in training. The same data and the same RL recipe lifted Prior-ACT from 60% to 82%, which suggests the limit is TurboVLA’s base representation rather than the data or the fine-tuning.

ROBOTIS, Jun – Aug 2026

Human-in-the-loop and HG-DAgger

Imitation learning never sees the robot’s own mistakes, so it never learns to recover. In HG-DAgger, a human takes over from the failing state and shows the correction. On RLinf, I tested HG-DAgger and HIL-SERL with GR00T N1.7 and built the ROS 2 teleoperation system around them for the AI Worker.

While the policy runs, the operator holds the leader arms: dragging the joystick down pauses the policy and hands over control, and pressing it hands control back. Base and head motion is locked out. Data is auto-labeled as demo, expert, or policy and re-weighted 0.6 / 0.3 / 0.1 for retraining, with the two seconds at each hand-off excluded, and saved in LeRobot format. A teleop UI in Cyclo Intelligence shows the task, a bar counting only real intervention, and each episode’s outcome. HIL-SERL was implemented but not fully validated.

GR00T letterboxes camera images, and its attention drifted to the black padding. Square-cropping the wrist cameras and padding the head camera fixed it, better than cropping to a region of interest. Wrist cameras mattered most for precise steps.

The AI Worker learned a long-horizon peg-and-screw task from only 31 real demos (GR00T N1.7, 30k steps on 2× H100) plus one HG-DAgger round of 13 intervention episodes (6k more steps on 2× A100), far fewer than the 100+ demos VLAs usually need. Over 30 trials, that one round raised peg-in-hole from 40% to 100%, grabbing the driver from 10% to 87%, screwing from 0% to 40%, and place-and-return, the last step, from 0% to 40% (100% of the trials that finished screwing), while grabbing the screw stayed at 100%, with emergent recovery such as re-finding the hole and checking that a peg was fully inserted.

Computer architecture, 2026

PDPM

A RISC-V processor that changes its own pipeline depth while it runs, steered by a perceptron.

IEEE ICCD 2026, first author

Accepted (26% acceptance)
PaperCodeCitation

The problem

Pipeline depth is fixed when a processor is designed, but the depth that runs a program fastest changes with the workload, and even between phases of one program. Deeper pipelines allow faster clocks but pay more for every branch; shallower ones do the opposite.

Approach

I built an RV32IM core as a seven-stage superset whose four bypass multiplexers collapse it to 3, 5, or 7 stages at runtime, with architectural state preserved. Every 200 instructions, a 10-weight int8 perceptron head reads four counters (how often branches flush the pipeline, how sensitive the program is to depth, how often loads stall, and how long the program has run), predicts two cycle ratios between depths, and picks the depth with the lowest predicted execution time. A confidence gate and a 16-entry phase memory stop it from switching back and forth.

Instead of a configuration label, the head learns the quantity the runtime actually optimizes. Because it predicts ratios, moving to a new clock plan changes two constants, with no retraining, and the same holds for energy. An ablation shows this is where the gain comes from: a 3-class depth classifier reaches 90.73%, and swapping its labels for the two ratio targets lifts it to 94.28% with fewer weights. Paired over 45 trials, that is 3.11 points more accurate (95% CI 1.78 to 4.48, p = 1.3×10⁻⁴), and its errors cost less, 167 at a mean 1.10% of execution time against 251 at 1.50%. A larger model does not help: a small MLP reaches only 71.70%.

The predictor fits in 478 LUTs, 0.75% of the Artix-7 FPGA. The core can also reconfigure its own clock through the FPGA’s clock manager, relocking in about 21.5 µs. The full artifact is open source, with 71 automated checks that reproduce every number in the paper.

Results

  • 94.28% configuration accuracy at +0.107% mean overhead on 301 benchmarks, with 10 weights instead of a 12-class classifier’s 60. Using only features a deployed chip can observe: 89.40% at +0.255%.
  • On 340 edge-inference programs (attention, GEMM, state-space models, VLA heads), it matched the per-phase oracle depth on 313 of 315 phases and finished 0.44% faster than the best fixed depth per program.
  • Against the strongest static configuration, always running five stages at 1.3× clock, PDPM cuts mean overhead from +2.57% to +0.107%. The static choice exceeds 5% overhead on 94 of 301 benchmarks (up to 48.7%); PDPM exceeds it on none.
  • Runs closed-loop on a Xilinx Artix-7 at 87.1 MHz with the three-depth head (the CPU was first brought up on a Lattice iCE40). Its committed state is byte-identical to a fixed-depth reference on all 641 programs.

Tested on 2026 edge AI

The deployment suite is 340 programs of 2026 edge-inference computation, written in C at microcontroller scale: 16 KB of memory and integer arithmetic only. 300 are kernels from 44 families, spanning attention, quantized dense algebra, convolution, state-space and recurrent sequence models, decoding, retrieval, and robotic action heads. The other 40 chain those kernels into ten model types at four shapes each, giving 315 labeled phases. The suite shares no program with the 301 benchmarks used to compare predictors.

The best depth depends on the workload and the clock plan together. At one clock, the 300 kernels split 286/14/0 across 3, 5, and 7 stages; under the analytical clock plan they split 8/73/219, led by attention, GEMV/GEMM, MoE routing, RoPE/YaRN, and the Mamba scan, which win by up to 11.3% over five stages. A wrong fixed depth costs up to 78.9% on a single kernel. A head fitted only on the isolated kernels follows the depth changes inside the 40 model programs it has never seen, settling within about 200 cycles, and matches the per-phase best depth on 313 of 315 phases. Letting the clock follow the depth is worth a further 5.8% on these kernels, and 5.1% once the measured relock is charged.

On the FPGA

On the Artix-7, the predictor drives the mode controller from the core’s own counters, with no host in the loop. The ordered sequence of every register write and store matched a fixed-depth reference on all 301 benchmarks, 300 kernels, and 40 model programs, at 10- and 40-cycle memory latency, across 144 to 1,315 self-initiated depth switches. A separate bitstream let the core retune its own clock alongside its depth through the MMCM, over 470 switches, with relock measured at 21.08 to 21.44 µs.

Retargeting the constants to measured power per mode (0.448, 0.365, and 0.413 mW/MHz) takes the closed loop from +0.454% to −0.265% of a per-program energy reference, with the weights untouched. On the twelve Embench IoT 1.0 programs that fit the runtime, accuracy holds at 93.3%. And because constant propagation removes any mode the frozen controller cannot reach, freezing the third output lets synthesis reclaim the seven-stage datapath, a 24% area reduction from changing ten weights.

My roleEverything from RTL to results: the Verilog core and controller, the benchmark suites, training and evaluation, and FPGA bring-up.

Brain-computer interfaces, 2026

iPhoneme

Turning brain signals into text for people with ALS.

arXiv preprint, equal-contribution first author

PaperCodearXivCitation

The problem

Roughly 173,000-232,500 people with ALS cannot speak, yet only 22-31 people have ever received a speech BCI implant. Decoders still make about one error per ten phonemes, and the low-cost alternative, eye-tracking keyboards, manages 5-10 words per minute because every glance risks an accidental click.

Approach

We trained on the T15 dataset: 45 sessions and 8,071 trials of 256-electrode recordings over four speech-motor areas, filtered, binned into 20 ms windows, and normalized per session. ConformerXL adapts the Conformer speech-recognition architecture to these 512-channel signals: a prenet of dilated convolutions and a bidirectional GRU corrects neural timing jitter, 8× subsampling stabilizes CTC training, and Pre-RMSNorm keeps training stable. A 6-gram phoneme language model (3.1M sequences from the CMU Dictionary, LibriSpeech, and T15) then corrects the output through WFST beam search, tuned over 150 Optuna trials.

Eye-tracking keyboards confuse pointing with selecting: every glance is a possible click (the “Midas touch” problem), so users must stare at a key for 500-1000 ms. “Slide to Unlock” separates the two, like holding Ctrl while clicking: gaze only points, and a silently spoken phoneme, detected by the same implant in 100-180 ms, confirms. That is 3-10× faster than dwell time, and a stray glance never clicks.

The phoneme also carries gestures. Holding a trigger such as “Oh” while sweeping your gaze swipes the screen: onset starts the gesture, the gaze path sets its direction and distance, and release ends it. A different trigger, “Ah”, anchors a text selection where you look, stretches it with your gaze, and copies it on release, which dwell-time interfaces cannot do.

A trigger phoneme must be detected reliably yet rarely spoken, or it fires mid-conversation. We scored every phoneme as precision × recall ÷ its frequency in natural speech, over 365M phonemes of LibriSpeech. The rarest (ZH, OY) lose because the decoder confuses them; the top five, DH, HH, Y, W, and TH, reach over 94% validation precision at under 3.5% of everyday speech. High-stakes actions, like sending a message or a medical decision, can require one specific high-confidence trigger.

Results

  • 92.14% phoneme accuracy and 73.39% word accuracy on the T15 dataset, about 3 points above the prior state of the art (89%).
  • Beam search added 2.76 points over greedy decoding, after we fixed a backoff bug that had made the language model worse than none.
  • Runs on a CPU at 180 ms latency.
  • Accuracy falls to about 80.8% on the 2025 sessions, which bring new vocabulary after a 7-month gap; continual learning is the next step.
My roleBuilt the model end to end: preprocessing, the ConformerXL decoder, and the language model and WFST decoder. For the gaze-phoneme interface, my teammate built the UI and backend, and we designed together which phonemes, combined with which gaze direction and location, trigger which actions.

Human-computer interaction, 2025

Watch-Out

A wearable safety system for people with Alzheimer’s who wander.

ACM CHI 2025 Extended Abstracts, Student Design Competition

1st Place (1 in 84)
PaperVideoCHI videoPosterSlidesDOICitation

The problem

Six in ten people with dementia wander, and about 30% of wandering cases end in death; the chance of being found alive drops to 20% after three to five days. Our interviews with the CEO of an elderly-care institute and a man caring for his mother showed the other side: caregivers have to be ready at every moment, and many can no longer live normal lives. The usual fixes do not help much. Patients take off GPS shoes and clothing, and locking the door keeps them safe only by isolating them, which can worsen their dementia. We wanted caregivers to live without constant worry, and people nearby to help a lost patient directly, instead of city-wide missing-person alerts that are vague and easy to ignore.

Approach

Watch-Out escalates one step at a time, and calling the authorities, who are often busy and may struggle to find a lost patient, is the last resort. The door lock lets the patient leave on purpose. Outside, geofencing and the watch track whether they stay in their safe zone. If they leave it, the watch’s digital compass guides them back; if they are still out, nearby bystanders are asked to help; if no one does, the caregiver steps in with QuickCall; and only then are the authorities called.

At the door, a smart lock allows a “conscious exit.” The patient can leave after two steps: a speaker asks short, calm questions (where are you going, what are you wearing?), and LED guides show where to tap their smartwatch on the NFC reader. Caregivers get the destination, exit time, and answers right away, can bypass the lock with a fingerprint, and exits are held back in extreme weather. Because the door opens only when the watch is tapped, the patient always leaves wearing it, and a watch is harder to take off than shoes or clothing.

The compass guides with vibration and voice. The bystander request is an SOS sent by AirDrop or Quick Share to phones within 10 m, with the patient’s appearance, what might upset them, and a caregiver contact, and first responders can tap the watch (QuickScan) for medical details. QuickCall lets the caregiver talk through the watch without the patient having to answer. If the watch comes off outside the home zone, it asks to be put back on, then sounds a louder alarm after three minutes.

The caregiver app shows location, vital signs and movement history at all times. Caregivers draw customizable safe zones directly on the map, from a wide area around home to one place such as a supermarket the patient often visits, and a heatmap shows the places and landmarks the patient has wandered to. Wandering arrives as a quiet notification; a real alarm, the kind that pulls a caregiver away from work, comes only when the patient is in danger or the situation reaches QuickCall.

Results

  • A design proposal grounded in interviews with a caregiver and the CEO of an elderly-care institute.
  • Won 1st Place (1 in 84) at the ACM CHI 2025 Student Design Competition in Yokohama.
  • Testing with patients is the next step.
My roleIdeation, 3D design, and the system and sensor design for the smart door lock and smartwatch, plus part of the code. Presented at CHI 2025 with another teammate.

IoT and disaster response, 2025

Smart Emergency Window

A security window that opens itself in any disaster situation.

Two JKSCI 2025 papers and a Korean patent application

Excellence Paper Award
Paper IPaper IICitations

The problem

Many people in Korea live in banjiha, semi-basement homes whose windows are covered with fixed security bars. Since 2022, people have died trapped behind those bars during flash floods and fires. The bars need tools to remove, and existing openable designs are manual, take up indoor space, or stop working when the power fails.

Approach

In Paper I, two parts of the window move at once. A motor lifts the upper frame on a rack-and-pinion, freeing the tops of the bars, while a metallic bevel gear rotates the lower cylindrical base. The bars and the rotating base are inclined, so a small turn lines every bar up with its opening and gravity swings them out, with enough torque and deceleration that no one outside gets hurt. The bars clear the window within three seconds.

If the power is cut or a motor fails, an LED next to the lever tells the occupant to release the window by hand: they push the release forward and up, and inclined surfaces pass that force on to free the upper frame. A spring mechanism holds the gear in place, so the bars stay released once the user lets go. The release is reachable only from inside, so it does not weaken the window’s security.

Flame, heat (triggering at about 57 °C), smoke, and multispectral flame sensors watch for fire, and conductivity and float sensors watch for water rising near the floor. A Raspberry Pi reads them and switches battery power to the upper and lower motors through an isolating relay and an L298N driver. A phone app shows live readings and controls the window over Bluetooth and MQTT, with sensor data logged to the cloud. If the network drops, the Pi keeps deciding locally; if the power fails, a backup battery runs it for 24 hours in low-power mode.

Paper II adds AI so the window can act before local sensors detect danger. A classifier learns the home’s normal sensor patterns and flags deviations early; a BERT model reads weather forecasts and government disaster alerts, using haversine distance and magnitude to judge whether a warning applies to this home; and a decision layer combines both with news broadcasts, weighing threat probability, urgency, time to impact, and harm. Redundant sensors cross-check each other, and when the AI is unsure, it asks for manual confirmation instead of acting.

Compared with the first prototype, the final version replaced the acrylic frame with plastic. The design called for spur gears, but the first prototype used bevel gears because they were what we could source; the next design moved to spur gears, which transmit power more efficiently, paired with stronger motors for more torque.

Results

  • Paper I: the bars release within three seconds, cutting evacuation time by 89.5% (84.2% in manual mode), with 98% reliability across 100 simulated fires and 100 of 100 manual releases, tested with elderly users, children, and users with limited mobility.
  • Held 97% reliability over about 150 repeated cycles and 95% from −10 to 50 °C.
  • Sensors detected flame at 95% accuracy within 5 m and smoke at 94% in enclosed spaces, read temperature to ±1 °C, and caught water-level changes as small as 1 cm (98%).
  • Paper II: threats detected 127 seconds earlier than conventional systems, with 98.7% decision accuracy (vs. 85.3% for sensor thresholds).
  • Excellence Paper Award for Paper I; Korean patent application filed in 2026.

Reinforcement learning, ongoing

IGEN

A parent that teaches a child agent across generations, and emotions that emerge because the child has a body to protect.

Intergenerational affective reinforcement learning, in progress

Initial slidesCode, first version (private)Code, current version (private)

Why

Each of the three things I think robots are missing has a counterpart in IGEN. The child knows food, hazards and its own moves only by what they do to its body, so a recolored or relabeled world can be mapped back onto itself instead of learned again. A body model predicts what each action will do to that body before the child moves, the way a gut feeling does. And the child’s internal state has no definition of its own: it is shaped only by having a body to protect and a parent to answer to, which is where emotions would have to come from. Worlds whose rules change test whether the child can leave a way of acting that no longer works. The parent is there because people learn all of this by being raised: a parent adjusts how a child explores, and over time the child learns to regulate itself.

First, a parent that sets the curriculum

A parent agent reads a child reinforcement-learning agent’s learning-progress signals (curiosity, frustration, and confidence, later eight in all) and sets its curriculum: reward shaping, how hard to explore, learning rate, planning horizon, and how much to seek novelty. When a generation ends, the child’s weights and the parent’s strategy carry into the next. I first trained the parent with the exact bilevel (Stackelberg) meta-gradient, verified against finite differences to about four digits, and later with REINFORCE on the child’s realized performance.

Exploration-forward curricula clearly beat plain PPO. On MinAtar, the learned parent raised Breakout from 12.3 to 22.8 and Space Invaders from 97.8 to 137.2. On full Atari Freeway (2 seeds), it held a return of 26.5 while plain PPO peaked and then collapsed to 0. But across games, the learned parent was within noise of a random one, and the hand-written heuristic won on Asterix.

Now, a child with a body

In the first version, the child’s “emotions” were eight statistics I computed from its training, such as return signal-to-noise and value loss. The current version replaces them with a state the child has to form itself, and gives it a body to form it around. The child lives in a 10×10 grid of food, hazards and walls. It has energy it must keep up, skin that senses contact on each side, warmth that rises near hazards, and damage on each of its four sides. It is rewarded only for keeping its hunger down.

There is no pain term in the reward. When a side is injured, moves in that direction fail, the senses on that side get noisier, and energy drains faster. I proved that the best achievable value can only fall as capacity is lost, so avoiding injury is worth learning without being told to. Enough damage on one side, or running out of energy, kills the child. It then sits out 20 steps and is reborn into a fresh layout, which costs the dynasty data and the parent reward.

The world also changes in ways the child cannot see. With no change in appearance, food sometimes restores only 40% as much energy and hazards sometimes hurt 1.75 times as much, and these hidden regimes switch at random, about every 500 steps. Moving burns twice the energy of resting, and food next to a hazard is worth double, so whether to rest, forage or take a risk depends on how hungry the child is and on what it believes about the world at that moment.

The child is four small modules, about 263k parameters under a one-million cap: perception, a body model that predicts what each of its five actions would do to its body, a slow internal state z, and an actor-critic. Hunger and injury reach the actor only through z, and no loss defines z. It is shaped only by acting and by what the parent needs to hear, because it is the only message the child sends to the parent.

The child also never sees what an object is. It knows each kind of object only by what touching it did to its body (energy gained, damage taken, pressure on the skin, whether it blocked the way), and each action only by where it actually went. These two tables have no learned weights and keep updating as the child lives, so its belief about the hidden world can be read out directly, and a relabeled world can be mapped back onto its own body instead of learned again.

The parent reads z and sets how the child trains: how much it explores, how fast it learns, and how far ahead it values. Each generation starts from the best child so far. In the latest version, each of the 256 children in a batch gets its own curriculum from its own z, because averaging z over the whole batch had washed out the state the parent needed to read.

Before any learning run, the world is checked bit for bit against a plain-Python reference (0 mismatches in 64,000 transitions), the gradient into z is checked against finite differences, and the emergence criteria were fixed before the first sweep.

What the first seven sweeps showed

  • The child approaches food 0.75-0.85 of the time when hungry and 0.44-0.50 when full, and that runs through z: shuffling z between children removes 87-100% of the difference, and clamping it passes the intervention test in up to 4 of 5 seeds.
  • The value the child assigns to food tracks the hidden nutrition regime at a correlation of −0.70 to −0.76 across the main runs, so its belief about the invisible world is accurate. Fragility is tracked only weakly, since hazards are touched about 15 times less often than food.
  • With the controls relabeled, survival recovers from 0.88 to 0.99 of baseline by the second visit, against 0.42 → 0.41 without body grounding. Recolored objects do not transfer yet: when food looks harmful, the child avoids it and never gets the contact that would correct the belief.
  • I am still developing the parent. Its current levers (how much to explore, how fast to learn, how far ahead to value) have a best setting that depends mostly on training time, so a carefully searched schedule still keeps up with a parent that reads the child. Giving each child its own curriculum already produced the best z so far: the most causal, the most informative beyond the body, and the most accurate about the hidden world.
  • When food appears only next to hazards, the child keeps about 80% of its normal survival, but when the rules flip entirely (food injures, hazards feed), it has not yet learned to switch strategy. That jump out of the system is what I am working toward, along with the strictest criterion I set before the first sweep: showing that what z carries beyond the current body comes from having something to lose.

What comes next

IGEN is still in development, and these results come from its first world. The parent, the child and the dynasty stay; I am changing what the parent controls and what the world asks of the child. Next is an uncertainty channel, so the child can doubt a belief and test it again, which the flipped-rule world requires; a re-run of deliberation, where the child chooses from z how far to think ahead and pays for it in energy; and levers whose right setting depends on the child rather than on the clock. Then the same body interface moves into 2D physics and then MuJoCo manipulation, where fingertip touch sensors become the skin and actuator strength becomes the capacity that injury takes away.
